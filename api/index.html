The brief

From our conversation, the brief is clear. The website needs to do more than present information. It needs to build trust, qualify prospects, and move the right people toward the right next step before a consultation ever happens.

The business has evolved beyond the current site. The offer is sharper, the positioning is more aligned with professional services than traditional real estate, and the audience is clearer: capable, time-poor professionals looking for guidance and confidence in a high-stakes decision.

One idea kept surfacing throughout the conversation: qualification earlier in the relationship, not later. That's the principle this proposal is built around.

What the website needs to do

Property investment is ultimately a trust business. Your clients are committing serious capital to a long-horizon decision, and the real question isn't "is this firm competent?" It's "do I trust Emma and API enough to guide a decision this important?"

Which means the website has a very different job. It's not a brochure. It's not a portfolio. It's a trust architecture, a system that does three things at once:

  1. Makes the first call feel like a second conversation, not a first.
  2. Qualifies prospects against your readiness scale, before anyone speaks to Chris.
  3. Routes them based on fit: into a booking, into education, or gently out.

Most agencies sell one piece of this. Design studios sell the aesthetic. Marketing teams sell the funnel. Automation specialists sell the wiring. Not many of them sell the way those pieces work together. That's the part that turns the website into a sales tool.

The kickoff won't open with a blank brief. The work breaks into six connected departments: strategy, copy, design, conversion, operations, and automation. Each plays a different role, but the value comes from how they work together.

The AI concierge

Worth its own section because it's one of the parts of the system that quietly does the most work, and because it sits outside the standard six departments.

What it is

A conversational AI assistant embedded on the website, available to any visitor who wants to ask a question rather than navigate to find the answer. It uses a chat-style interface, but designed and behaved like a concierge, not a chatbot widget. Discreet, opt-in, on-brand. No pushy popups, no demanding attention, no canned greetings. Just available when wanted.

Why it fits API specifically

Property investment is a high-question category. Prospects want to know about the process, fees, timelines, property types, suburb-specific commentary, what makes a good deal, how long settlement takes, what happens if they want to exit early. A conversational interface is genuinely good UX for that kind of exploratory Q&A. It's better than asking them to dig through pages or wait for a call.

It also extends the trust architecture. Visitors who aren't ready to book a call can still engage with the business in a meaningful way, getting real, accurate answers grounded in API's own content, voice, and perspective.

That moves the relationship forward without requiring a human at the other end of every conversation.

What it's trained on

Your existing content library: podcast transcripts, eBooks, articles, webinar recordings, market updates. The assistant answers in your voice, cites your own material, and stays within what API has actually said. If a question falls outside what the content covers, the assistant routes the visitor to a human conversation rather than guessing.

How it differs from a chatbot widget

Most chatbot widgets are designed to capture leads. They pop up, demand attention, route to a sales rep. The concierge is designed to serve trust. It only appears when invited, it reads as part of the site rather than bolted onto it, and its primary measure of success is whether the visitor felt their question was actually answered. Lead capture happens as a natural next step when the conversation suggests readiness, not as the goal of every interaction.

Findability

How prospects discover API is changing. Traditional Google search still matters, but AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Google's AI overviews) are increasingly where buyers research advisors before they ever land on a website. You've already seen this firsthand: a client recently found you through ChatGPT.

A website rebuild is the right moment to address both. The underlying work is similar (structured content, clear expertise signals, technical hygiene), but the output reaches two different discovery channels.

Where API gets found today

A review of the current site's discoverability before any structural decisions get made. For traditional search: which pages are pulling traffic, which keywords API ranks for, where the structural gaps are. For AI tools: how API currently surfaces (or doesn't) in queries a prospect might actually ask, like "who are the best buyers' agents in Sydney," "how does SMSF property investing work," "what does Active Property Investing do." This isn't a generic technical scan. It's a strategic read of where the business already has visibility, so the rebuild protects and extends it rather than starting from zero.

What we'd fix in the build

The current site has visible weak spots a rebuild is the right moment to address. Generic page titles, content trapped inside videos and PDFs that neither search engines nor AI systems can read, no dedicated landing pages for high-intent searches (SMSF investing, dual-occupancy strategy, region-specific queries), and shallow internal linking that makes deeper content hard to find.

The new site structure, copy, and on-page setup are designed around these findings. Critically, content gets structured so it can be cited: clear question-and-answer formats, named expertise indicators, schema markup that helps AI systems understand who API is and what you do. The goal is to make API more likely to be the answer when an AI is asked, without overpromising specific outcomes. No-one can guarantee placement in AI search results; what we can do is set the conditions that make recommendation far more likely.

Migration and continuity

Every URL on the existing site gets mapped to its new destination, with redirect rules in place before launch. That continuity work helps preserve existing search visibility and ensures the transition into the new site happens cleanly, without losing the value already built into the current structure.

The same structural foundations also support continuity in how AI systems discover, interpret, and reference the site over time.

Ongoing SEO management, monthly keyword reporting, and link building campaigns sit outside this proposal. They're separate engagements if and when you want them, ideally folded into Phase 3 once the foundation is in place.

How we'd approach it

Three phases. The first two build the system. The third optimises and evolves it.

Why phasing produces a better system

The phased approach allows the system to mature over time. Some parts of the website genuinely improve when they're built on top of real usage, behaviour, and data.

Each phase builds on the one before it, allowing the system to become sharper and more effective as it evolves.

A few concrete examples:

The readiness quiz. Phase 1 ships a solid lead qualification form, covering the four readiness dimensions and routing visitors toward booking, nurture, or filter outcomes. That's enough to begin qualifying leads from day one.

Phase 2 evolves that into a more interactive experience, with adaptive questions, scoring, and more personalised routing. The second version benefits from seeing how real visitors move through the system first, allowing the experience to become more informed and effective over time.

The same applies to follow-up and nurture communication. Real usage reveals which questions prospects keep asking, which content builds trust, and where people need more clarity before moving forward.

Phasing allows the system to evolve from real behaviour rather than assumptions, making each stage more informed than the last.
